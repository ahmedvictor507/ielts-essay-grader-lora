The question of whether higher education should serve primarily as vocational preparation or as a vehicle for scholarly enquiry reflects a deeper tension about what universities are for. Although the employment-oriented view has understandable appeal, I contend that institutions serve students and society best by treating rigorous academic learning as the foundation upon which practical competence is built.

Advocates of vocational focus have a compelling case. Tuition fees are substantial, and graduates who emerge without marketable skills may reasonably feel that they have been poorly served. Programmes that embed placements, studio work or clinical practice demonstrably shorten the transition into employment, and in fields such as nursing or software engineering, hands-on proficiency is not optional but essential.

Nevertheless, a curriculum designed solely around present employer demand is inherently short-sighted. Industries are being reshaped by automation at a pace that makes today's specific techniques obsolete within a decade. What endures is the capacity to reason analytically, to evaluate evidence and to learn independently, all of which are honed through disciplined engagement with theory. A graduate who grasps why a method works can retrain when the method is superseded, whereas one trained narrowly cannot.

The most defensible position, therefore, is not an either-or choice but a sequenced one: theoretical depth first, deliberately paired with applied experience so that concepts are tested against reality. Universities that achieve this will produce adaptable professionals rather than merely job-ready ones.

In sum, while practical training is indispensable, it is academic rigour that gives it lasting value, and institutions should be judged by how well they integrate the two.
