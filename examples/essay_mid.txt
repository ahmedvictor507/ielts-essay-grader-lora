The role of universities has long been debated. While some argue that they should prepare students directly for the workplace, others maintain that academic knowledge remains their core purpose. This essay will examine both perspectives before explaining why a balance is preferable.

Those who favour practical training point out that graduates often struggle to find work because they lack real-world experience. Employers increasingly expect new staff to be productive immediately, so courses that include internships, laboratory work and project-based learning can make students more employable. For instance, many engineering graduates who have completed an industrial placement receive job offers faster than those who have not.

On the other hand, academic knowledge provides the foundation on which all skills are built. Subjects such as mathematics, history or philosophy develop critical thinking and the ability to analyse complex problems, which are valuable in any profession. Furthermore, technology changes quickly, and a student who understands underlying principles can adapt more easily than one who has only learned a specific tool.

In my opinion, universities should combine both approaches. Theoretical study should remain central, but it ought to be complemented by opportunities to apply knowledge in realistic settings. This would produce graduates who think deeply and can also perform well at work.

In conclusion, neither practical skills nor academic knowledge should be neglected, and the best degree programmes are those that integrate them.
